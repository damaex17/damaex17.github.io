AI as an attacker: agentic offensive frameworks

Germany’s Federal Office for Information Security (BSI) now rates the threat level as “tense to critical”, the first time it has gone that far. Autonomous attack frameworks are a big part of the reason, and they aren’t picky about the size of their targets.

Most people in IT made their peace a while ago with the fact that criminals use large language models. Phishing emails got better grammar, malicious scripts got written faster. Annoying, but the basic picture stayed the same: a human was still running the attack.

That is the part that’s changing now. So-called agentic offensive frameworks (AOFs) let an operator name a target and step back while a group of AI agents does the actual work. A year ago this would have sounded like a conference keynote about the distant future. Since late summer, it has been turning up in incident reports.

What happened this autumn

On November 19, the BSI published its annual report on the state of IT security in Germany and raised its overall assessment from “tense” to “tense to critical”. The main driver, according to the agency, is AI that finds vulnerabilities and hands over a working exploit along with them. The report also says many companies and consumers feel safer than they actually are. BSI president Claudia Plattner was blunt about what comes next: “In the next stage, we expect more and more attacks by autonomous AI agents.”

Strictly speaking, that next stage has already started. The report covers the period up to mid-2026, and a lot has happened since. On November 13, the FBI issued a FLASH alert about ARTEX, an open-source framework originally built for penetration testing. According to the Bureau, malicious actors have been using it since at least August against financial institutions, government agencies and tech companies, and some intrusions reached privileged access in under three hours. CERT-EU, which looks after the security of the EU institutions, had warned about ARTEX-assisted attacks about a week earlier.

The most visible case so far is the European Commission. On November 19 it confirmed that a campaign detected in October involved a publicly available AI tool, and Politico reported that CERT-EU had identified ARTEX. Systems for payroll, leave requests and medical reimbursements were disrupted for more than ten days, partly because the Commission took applications offline as a precaution.

Online retail has its own example. In September, Gambit Security described an operator who used a framework called Cairn, together with two other AI tools, against online stores. Gambit confirmed at least 27 compromised companies, and more than a hundred further websites were linked to the campaign. One detail from Gambit’s findings deserves more attention than it got: the attacker’s automated cleanup routine destroyed data at some of the victims. So even an intrusion that isn’t aimed at destruction can end with data gone.

The names will change. ARTEX and Cairn are this year’s examples, and next year there will be others. What matters is the approach behind them.

How an attack runs when nobody is at the keyboard

The basic idea is simple. A human operator supplies a target and a goal, something like “get into company X and collect everything you can find about product Y.” From there, a swarm of LLM agents takes over. One breaks the job into tasks, others run scanners and exploit tools, the results get evaluated and the plan gets adjusted. The loop keeps running until the goal is reached or the operator calls it off.

Rich Mogull of the Cloud Security Alliance put it soberly: tools like ARTEX aren’t magic. They do roughly what a competent penetration tester does, only automated and fast. That alone is bad enough. A human attacker gets bored, runs out of time or doesn’t know a particular system well. Agents with the right tools have none of those limits. They check every exposed service and try every weakness they find. Once inside, they keep the same pace: file shares, old test servers, the admin panel someone set up in 2019 and forgot about. The map of the network they end up with can be more complete than the one the IT team has. The only real limit is computing power. Working hours don’t come into it.

Attribution is another headache. The agents write fresh exploit code as they go, change tactics from one loop to the next and rotate IP addresses through VPNs or the Tor network. Classic indicators of compromise lose a lot of their value when the attacker rebuilds its tooling on every run. Linking an incident to a specific framework is hard. Linking it to a specific group is harder still.

Can we just switch everything off over the holidays?

Among IT leaders, one question keeps coming up: how do you defend against an opponent that never gets tired? And what are administrators at small and midsize businesses supposed to do, when many of them are already stretched thin?

That second group shouldn’t count on being too small to notice. Automation makes each additional target cheap for the attacker, so smaller companies become worth a try. According to the BSI report, around 90 percent of ransomware cases already hit small and midsize businesses.

For the coming weeks, security experts suggest something pragmatic: where possible, take vulnerable systems out of reach over the holidays. If a service doesn’t need to be reachable from the internet between Christmas and New Year, shut it down or put it behind a VPN. Fewer people are watching during that stretch, and an agent swarm doesn’t take time off.

That’s a stopgap, though, and there’s no quick long-term fix. The first step is broader awareness that the threat has changed. The harder step is turning that awareness into real changes in how organizations defend themselves.

Defensive minimalism

The approach that holds up best is attack surface reduction, or what you might call defensive minimalism. It comes with a rule of thumb that is easy to say and uncomfortable to apply: anyone who wants to switch on a feature should be able to explain why it’s worth defending for the entire lifetime of the service.

In practice, that means making the attack surface as small as possible and then hardening everything that remains. Every exposed port, login page, API and legacy interface is something an agent will find and probe, so each one needs a reason to exist. It also means finally paying off the technical debt that has piled up over the years: multifactor authentication on every account rather than just the ones someone remembered, old protocols switched off, admin interfaces taken off the public internet.

Network segmentation deserves its own mention. Once an agent is inside, its thoroughness really pays off for the attacker, because it will try every route to every other system. Clean segmentation keeps one compromised machine from turning into a company-wide incident.

If all that sounds like a multi-year program, it is. So start with the quick wins. Make sure you can actually respond to an incident: who gets called, who is allowed to isolate systems, where the offline backups are and whether anyone has tested restoring them. Then close the easiest ways in. Agents go for the low-hanging fruit just like human attackers do. They just find it faster.

No new tricks

There’s no clever countermeasure on the horizon that makes this problem go away, and it’s worth being skeptical of anyone who promises one. What works against an automated attacker is what has always worked: a solid, hardened foundation with as little exposed as possible. What has changed is the grace period. Systems that were safe mainly because nobody would bother with them are now being checked by something that bothers with everything.
